We propose FRAME, a four-quadrant multi-stage pretraining strategy that organizes pretraining into four stages to achieve four pronounced drops in loss. FRAME builds on two key findings: training first on high-PPL data and then on low-PPL data, and training first on low perplexity-difference (PD) data and then on high-PD data, each produce two significant loss drops and corresponding performance gains. By partitioning data into four quadrants and organizing them strategically, FRAME delivers a 16.8% average improvement on MMLU and CMMLU for a 3B model over conventional pretraining.

Motivation
------

Multi-stage pretraining is an appealing idea with a weak foundation: existing schemes partition data by intuitive heuristics and offer no quantitative criteria for where the boundaries should fall. Worse, the features typically used — text length, rare words, syntactic complexity — encode *human* notions of difficulty. What matters is what the model finds hard, so the partitioning metrics should be perceptible to the model itself. The broader literature also concentrates on *selecting* data while largely ignoring how data traits should align with learning stages.

Method
------

**Two model-perceptible metrics.** Two reference models are trained on an i.i.d. subset of the target corpus — a 100M weak model and a 1.3B strong model. Perplexity (PPL) under the strong model measures model–data fit. Perplexity difference is the relative gap, PD(x) = [PPL_weak(x) − PPL_strong(x)] / PPL_weak(x): low PD means both models learn the sample comparably, high PD means it is hard for the weak model specifically.

**Four quadrants.** Thresholds are chosen so the quadrants hold roughly equal token counts — a PPL split first, then a PD threshold within each half:

| Quadrant | PPL | PD | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | low | low | fit well by both reference models |
| Q2 | low | high | strong model handles it, weak model struggles |
| Q3 | high | low | poorly learned by both |
| Q4 | high | high | poorly learned by both, worse by the weak model |

**Stage ordering.** Two preliminary experiments supply the constraints. High-PPL → low-PPL beats random decisively (MMLU 39.6 / CMMLU 42.6 against 24.8 / 25.6), while the reverse order barely moves; low-PD → high-PD gives a smaller but consistent gain. So PPL should be non-increasing across stages and PD non-decreasing. Only two orderings are compatible with a double-loop construction, and each violates one constraint at the second-to-third boundary. Because the PPL effect dominates, we prioritize that constraint, giving **Q3 → Q4 → Q1 → Q2**. Ablations confirm the choice: the alternative ordering falls *below* random and was halted at stage three.

**Stage smoothing.** Abrupt transitions cause performance fluctuations, so consecutive stages are blended with an S-shaped mixing schedule, applied hierarchically — Q3 with Q4, Q1 with Q2, then the two halves — drawing in original order to preserve the global sequence. All processing is offline, and FRAME reorders rather than filters, so no data is discarded and training continuity is untouched.

Experiments
------

Main runs train a 3B model (Llama architecture) on 1T tokens, 500B Chinese and 500B English, spanning books, blogs, patents, Common Crawl and Wikipedia; a 1.3B model on 100B SlimPajama tokens validates transfer across corpora. Training uses Megatron on 512 Ascend 910B NPUs for 180+ hours per 3B run. Baselines include Random, PDPC, sequential sorting by PPL/PD/QuRating in both directions, and preference-curriculum variants.

| 3B / 1T tokens | MMLU | CMMLU | CEVAL | AVG (8 tasks) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random | 27.7 | 27.5 | 27.2 | 43.3 |
| PDPC | — | — | — | 45.6 |
| **FRAME** | **43.0** (+15.3) | **45.7** (+18.2) | **44.0** (+16.8) | **49.4** (+6.1) |

The headline **16.8% average improvement on MMLU and CMMLU** is the mean of +15.3 and +18.2. At 1.3B, FRAME tops every baseline with a 4.1-point average gain over random, on a different corpus — evidence the strategy is not corpus-specific.

Insights
------

- **Four loss drops, four capability jumps.** The loss curve shows four declines that align with benchmark improvements. Benchmarks stay flat early, then rise sharply after roughly 90K steps — consistent with foundational skills being acquired first and capability emerging once they are in place.
- **PPL and PD do different jobs.** PPL dominates loss magnitude; at matched PPL, higher-PD data converges to lower final loss. Low-PD data plateaus because weak and strong models fit it alike, while high-PD data keeps teaching new features as capacity grows — but converges slowly if introduced too early.
- **PD preserves diversity where PPL would not.** PPL varies sharply by domain (Wikipedia low, Reddit high), so sorting by PPL alone would make each stage domain-homogeneous. PD is near-normally distributed and similar across sources, so it partitions without collapsing diversity. Q3 is especially balanced, suiting it to stage one; Q2 concentrates complex, knowledge-rich text, suiting it to the end — which explains the outsized gains on knowledge benchmarks.
- **Model-perceived difficulty is orthogonal to human quality judgments.** Quality raters give similar distributions across quadrants, and semantic embeddings show no clear separation. No stage is starved of good data — difficulty and quality are genuinely different axes.
- **Grouping similar data stabilizes optimization.** Spectral analysis of the loss curve puts FRAME's high-frequency energy share at 0.02%, below both random and PDPC.

**Limitations.** Each dimension is split only in two; finer partitions are untested. Results cover dense transformers — Mamba and MoE architectures remain to be examined.

This work builds on [PDPC]({{ site.baseurl }}/publication/2025-07-27-preference-curriculum), which introduced the perplexity-difference metric.
